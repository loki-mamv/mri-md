Can You Eat or Drink Before an MRI?

September 16, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: For most MRI scans, yes. You can eat, drink, and take your usual medicines unless your imaging center tells you otherwise. The exceptions are some abdominal scans, bowel studies with oral contrast, cardiac stress MRIs (no caffeine), and any scan done with sedation or anesthesia. Fasting rules vary by facility, so the instructions from your own center always come first.

Which MRI scans let you eat and drink normally?

A brain, spine, knee, shoulder, or other joint MRI usually needs no food or drink restrictions. The machine images tissue with a magnet and radio waves, and a meal in your stomach does not change a picture of your head or your knee.

RadiologyInfo.org, the patient site run by the American College of Radiology (ACR) and the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA), puts it plainly. Eating and drinking guidelines "vary between specific exams and facilities," and you should take food and medications as usual unless your doctor says otherwise.

A few practical points still apply on a no-fasting day:

  • A light meal is a good idea. Many scans last 30 minutes or more, and hunger makes it harder to lie still. See how long an MRI takes for typical times by body part.
  • Use the restroom right before you go in. A full bladder is uncomfortable when you cannot move.
  • Read your appointment letter anyway. Some centers use one fasting policy for every scan to keep scheduling simple.

Why do some abdominal scans ask for an empty stomach?

Scans of the liver, gallbladder, pancreas, and bile ducts are the most common reason you may be asked to skip food. MedlinePlus, from the National Library of Medicine, notes that you may be asked not to eat or drink anything for 4 to 6 hours before an MRI.

The reason is image quality, not safety. Food and drink stimulate the gut and gallbladder, and movement or extra fluid near these organs can make the pictures harder to read.

That matters most for MRCP (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography), an MRI of the bile and pancreatic ducts. The RadiologyInfo.org MRCP page does not give one number. It says eating and drinking guidelines vary between facilities, so call your center if your letter is unclear.

MR enterography, an MRI of the small bowel often used for Crohn's disease, adds another step. You fast first, then drink a large volume of oral contrast so the bowel loops fill and stretch. In one 2017 study in Abdominal Radiology, patients fasted for 4 hours, then drank 1,600 mL of a 2% mannitol solution over 45 to 60 minutes before the scan. Other hospitals use different drinks, volumes, and timing. The RadiologyInfo.org MR enterography page again says eating and drinking rules vary between facilities.

Does gadolinium contrast mean you have to fast?

No, not by itself. Many centers once asked patients to fast before any contrast injection in case of nausea or vomiting. The evidence has not supported that habit for modern agents.

The ACR Manual on Contrast Media states that fasting "is not required prior to routine intravascular contrast material administration." It lists its reasons:

  • Gadolinium-based contrast used for MRI carries a much lower risk of vomiting than older, high-osmolality contrast agents.
  • In a 2009 study of 158,439 gadolinium injections, only 0.03% caused mild reactions such as nausea, vomiting, or rash.
  • Data show no protective effect of fasting on nausea, vomiting, or aspiration with modern contrast.
  • Fasting has its own harms, including low blood sugar in people with diabetes.

The same ACR section adds one exception. If you receive sedation, the anesthesia fasting rules apply. For more on the injection itself, read our guide to MRI with contrast.

Why avoid caffeine before a cardiac stress MRI?

A cardiac stress MRI uses a medicine to widen the heart's arteries while the scanner looks for areas with poor blood flow. Common choices, adenosine and regadenoson, act on adenosine receptors. Caffeine blocks those same receptors.

The FDA label for regadenoson (Lexiscan) tells patients to avoid products with methylxanthines for at least 12 hours before stress imaging. That list includes caffeinated coffee, tea, other caffeinated drinks, caffeine-containing medicines, and theophylline. The label covers nuclear stress tests. Using regadenoson for cardiac MRI is off-label.

A 2017 study of 98 patients found that coffee within 4 hours blunted the stress response to adenosine during cardiac MRI. It did not blunt the response to regadenoson in that study. Your center will not always know in advance which drug you will get, so the safe plan is simple. Follow its caffeine window, and check your medicine labels for caffeine. The RadiologyInfo.org cardiac MRI page confirms that food and drink rules vary between facilities.

What changes if you get sedation or general anesthesia?

This is where fasting becomes a safety rule. Sedation relaxes the reflexes that keep stomach contents out of your lungs. An empty stomach lowers the risk of aspiration.

The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) fasting guideline applies to general anesthesia, regional anesthesia, and procedural sedation. For healthy patients having elective procedures, it recommends these minimum times:

  • Clear liquids: 2 hours. Examples include water, pulp-free fruit juice, carbonated drinks, clear tea, and black coffee.
  • Breast milk: 4 hours.
  • Infant formula: 6 hours.
  • Nonhuman milk or a light meal (such as toast and a clear liquid): 6 hours.
  • Fried or fatty foods, or meat: 8 hours or more may be needed.

The ASA updated parts of this guideline in 2023, including pediatric fasting times, and it says local policies may differ. Many hospitals set longer windows.

Children are the largest group affected. RadiologyInfo.org notes that infants and young children often need sedation or anesthesia to hold still. The hospital will give you a cutoff time for food, milk, and clear liquids. Follow it exactly, since a child who ate too recently may have the scan postponed. The same applies to adults sedated for severe MRI claustrophobia under an anesthesia team. If you take a mild sedative prescribed by your own doctor, RadiologyInfo.org says not to drive yourself home.

What about your medications and diabetes?

Keep taking your regular medicines unless told otherwise. That is the default on the RadiologyInfo.org body MRI page.

Diabetes needs a plan if you are asked to fast. The ACR contrast manual names low blood sugar as a real harm of unnecessary fasting. If your scan requires an empty stomach, ask your prescriber how to adjust insulin or other glucose-lowering drugs. Also ask for an early morning slot, and bring a snack for afterward.

GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide slow stomach emptying. That matters if you will receive sedation or anesthesia. A 2024 multisociety guidance that includes the ASA says patients without a higher risk of aspiration may continue these drugs. For people at higher risk of delayed stomach emptying, it describes a liquid diet for at least 24 hours before the procedure. Tell the scheduler you take one, so the care team can decide. The same liquid-diet approach is familiar to anyone who has prepped for a colonoscopy, covered in depth at colonoscopy.md.

The bottom line

Most MRIs need no fasting. Plan on an empty stomach for some liver, gallbladder, and bile duct scans, often 4 to 6 hours. Expect oral contrast for MR enterography and a caffeine-free window before a cardiac stress MRI. Sedation or anesthesia brings the strictest rules: at least 2 hours for clear liquids and 6 hours for a light meal under the ASA guideline. Gadolinium contrast alone is not a reason to fast. Every center sets its own policy, so read your prep letter and call if it is unclear.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drink water before an MRI?

Usually yes. Water is allowed before most MRI scans, and it is a clear liquid under anesthesia fasting rules, which allow clear liquids up to 2 hours before sedation. Some abdominal and bowel scans restrict all drinks for a few hours, so check your prep letter.

Why are my MRI prep instructions different from a friend's?

The body part, the use of contrast or sedation, and the facility all change the rules. RadiologyInfo.org notes that eating and drinking guidelines vary between exams and facilities. Your own center's letter is the one to follow.

What happens if I ate before an MRI that required fasting?

Tell the technologist before the scan starts. For an abdominal MRI, the team may still scan you or may reschedule if image quality would suffer. For a scan with sedation or anesthesia, the team will often delay or reschedule to lower the risk of aspiration.

Can I eat right after my MRI?

Yes. MedlinePlus notes that you can resume your normal diet, activity, and medicines after an MRI. If you had sedation, wait until you are fully awake and follow the recovery team's instructions.

Should I stop my blood pressure or thyroid pills before an MRI?

Usually not. RadiologyInfo.org advises taking regular medications as usual unless you are told otherwise. Ask your doctor or the imaging center if you are unsure, especially if you will be sedated.
