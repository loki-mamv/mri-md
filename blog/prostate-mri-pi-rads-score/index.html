What Is a Prostate MRI and What Does a PI-RADS Score Mean?

The short answer: A prostate MRI is a detailed scan that looks for areas likely to be serious prostate cancer. Radiologists grade each spot with a PI-RADS score from 1 to 5. Per the 2019 PI-RADS v2.1 update, 1 means significant cancer is highly unlikely and 5 means it is highly likely.

What is a prostate MRI, and why do doctors order one?

A prostate MRI uses a strong magnet, radio waves, and a computer to make detailed pictures of the prostate. It uses no radiation. RadiologyInfo, run by the American College of Radiology and the Radiological Society of North America, says doctors use it to evaluate prostate cancer and to see whether it has spread. It can also help with infection or an enlarged prostate.

The most common reason today is a raised PSA blood test or an abnormal rectal exam. The scan helps decide two things: whether you need a biopsy, and where the needle should go. MRI can find tumors, but only a biopsy confirms cancer. Our guide on whether an MRI can detect cancer explains why.

The goal is to find clinically significant cancer. The 2023 AUA/SUO early detection guideline defines this as Grade Group 2 or higher. Grade Group 1 cancers are slow growing. Many men with them can be watched with active surveillance instead of treated.

What does each PI-RADS score from 1 to 5 mean?

PI-RADS stands for Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System. The radiologist scores each spot they see. The whole exam then gets the highest score of any single spot. A 2022 meta-analysis of PI-RADS v2.1 (17 studies, 1,268 patients) measured how often each score turned out to be significant cancer on biopsy. Here is each score, with that patient-level rate and what usually happens next:

  1. PI-RADS 1 (very low). Significant cancer is highly unlikely. About 6% of patients had it. Most men skip biopsy and keep up PSA checks.
  2. PI-RADS 2 (low). Significant cancer is unlikely. About 9% had it. Biopsy is usually skipped, unless other risk factors are high.
  3. PI-RADS 3 (intermediate). The finding is equivocal, meaning unclear. About 16% had significant cancer. Your urologist weighs PSA density, age, and family history. Some men have a biopsy. Others repeat the PSA or MRI later.
  4. PI-RADS 4 (high). Significant cancer is likely. About 59% had it. A targeted biopsy of the spot is the usual next step.
  5. PI-RADS 5 (very high). Significant cancer is highly likely. About 85% had it. Expect a targeted biopsy, often with extra systematic cores.

These are averages across many centers. The AUA guideline pooled 23 studies and found somewhat lower rates: 7% for scores 1 or 2, 11% for 3, 37% for 4, and 70% for 5. The guideline tells doctors to read scores in light of their own center's experience. Results vary with the scanner, the reader, and the patient group.

What happens after you get your PI-RADS score?

The AUA guideline treats PI-RADS 3 to 5 as an abnormal MRI. It notes that some clinicians use 4 to 5 as the cutoff instead, based on local expertise. The main guideline statements are:

  • Suspicious spot, first biopsy: clinicians should do targeted biopsies of the spot. They may also take a standard systematic set of cores.
  • Clear MRI but high risk: clinicians should still do a systematic biopsy. A "negative" MRI (PI-RADS 1 to 2) missed significant cancer in about 1 in 10 men in the guideline's review of 42 studies.
  • Negative biopsy: clinicians should not stop screening based on one negative biopsy alone.

Your report goes to the doctor who ordered it. They will share it with you. Our article on how long MRI results take covers typical wait times.

Does an MRI before biopsy actually help?

The key trial is PRECISION, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2018. It randomized 500 men who had never had a biopsy. One group had MRI first, with a targeted biopsy only if the scan was suspicious. The other group had a standard 10 to 12 core ultrasound-guided biopsy.

  • 28% of the MRI group had a non-suspicious scan and skipped biopsy.
  • Significant cancer was found in 38% of the MRI group versus 26% of the standard biopsy group.
  • The MRI group had 13 percentage points fewer diagnoses of insignificant cancer.

The AUA guideline rates MRI before a first biopsy as a conditional recommendation. It says clinicians "may use" it to find more Grade Group 2 or higher cancer. The guideline notes the benefit is clearest for men with a prior negative biopsy.

Will you need contrast dye for a prostate MRI?

Not always. A multiparametric MRI has three parts: T2-weighted images, diffusion-weighted images, and a series taken after IV gadolinium contrast. A biparametric MRI drops the contrast series. It is shorter and cheaper.

The PRIME trial (JAMA, 2025) tested this in 490 men across 22 centers in 12 countries. Biparametric MRI found significant cancer in 29.2% of men. The full contrast scan found it in 29.6%. The authors concluded the shorter scan was noninferior, provided image quality is adequate. Many centers still use contrast, so ask which type you will have. Our guide to MRI with contrast explains the IV and gadolinium safety.

How should you prepare for a prostate MRI?

Preparation varies between centers. RadiologyInfo says eating and drinking rules differ by facility. Follow the instructions your imaging center gives you. A typical checklist looks like this:

  1. Screening form. Tell the center about pacemakers, implants, metal fragments, kidney problems, and any past contrast reaction. The center checks kidney function before giving contrast.
  2. Medicines. Take your regular medicines as usual unless your doctor says otherwise.
  3. Ejaculation. Some centers ask you to avoid ejaculation for about 3 days. A 2017 Cambridge study found the seminal vesicles shrank about 26% one day after ejaculation. The authors supported 3 days of abstinence.
  4. Bowel prep. Gas and stool in the rectum can blur the diffusion images. A 2019 review on patient preparation lists bowel movement and rectal distension as key factors. Some centers ask you to empty your bowels or use an enema beforehand.
  5. Endorectal coil. Some exams use a thin coil placed a short distance into the rectum. Ask in advance whether your center uses one.
  6. Anxiety. If you are claustrophobic, ask your doctor about a mild sedative before the day.

The bottom line

A prostate MRI helps decide whether you need a biopsy and where to aim it. PI-RADS 1 and 2 mean significant cancer is unlikely. PI-RADS 3 is a gray zone. PI-RADS 4 and 5 usually lead to a targeted biopsy. The score is a risk estimate, not a diagnosis. Talk through your score with your urologist alongside your PSA, prostate size, and family history.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a PI-RADS 3 result cancer?

No. A PI-RADS 3 score means the finding is unclear. In a 2022 meta-analysis, about 16% of patients with a PI-RADS 3 result had clinically significant cancer on biopsy. Your urologist will weigh other factors such as PSA density before deciding on a biopsy.

Can a prostate MRI miss cancer?

Yes. The 2023 AUA guideline found that a negative MRI (PI-RADS 1 or 2) missed significant cancer in about 1 in 10 men who had never had a biopsy. That is why men at high risk may still get a systematic biopsy after a clear scan.

Does a PI-RADS 5 score mean I definitely have cancer?

No, but the risk is high. In the 2022 PI-RADS v2.1 meta-analysis, about 85% of patients with a PI-RADS 5 result had clinically significant cancer. Only a biopsy can confirm the diagnosis.

Is a prostate MRI painful?

The scan itself does not hurt, and it uses no radiation. You lie still inside the machine while it makes loud knocking sounds. If your center uses an endorectal coil or an IV for contrast, you may feel brief pressure or a needle stick.

Who scores the PI-RADS on my prostate MRI?

A radiologist reads the images and assigns the score. The AUA guideline says radiologists should use PI-RADS when they report prostate MRI. Your urologist then uses the score to plan next steps with you.
