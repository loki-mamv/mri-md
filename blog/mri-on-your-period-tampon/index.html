Can You Have an MRI on Your Period or With a Tampon In?

November 7, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes. Having your period is not an MRI safety problem, and you can usually keep a tampon, menstrual cup, or pad in. Per the FDA's tampon safety page (updated September 2026), tampons are cotton, rayon, or a blend. The item to watch is clothing, including period underwear, with hidden metal threads.

Is it safe to have an MRI on your period?

Yes. An MRI uses a strong magnet and radio waves. Menstrual bleeding does not interact with either one, so it does not raise your risk.

The real MRI hazards are metal and heat. The FDA says it receives around 300 adverse event reports for MRI scanners and coils each year. Most describe heating or burns, and second-degree burns are the most common patient problem. Those burns come from metal in implants, devices, and clothing, not from your body's normal processes.

The RadiologyInfo MRI safety page from the ACR and RSNA lists the things staff screen for: implants, devices, and clothing with metal. Menstruation is not on that list. You do not need to reschedule a scan because your period started, unless your own doctor asked for a specific cycle day (more on that below).

Can you keep a tampon, menstrual cup, or pad in during the scan?

In most cases, yes. Here is what each product is made of:

  • Tampons. The FDA says authorized tampons are made of cotton, rayon, or a blend of the two. The FDA also notes that very small amounts of metals can be detected in both regular and organic tampons. That statement is about what your body might absorb. Tampons have no metal parts, and we found no report of a tampon heating during an MRI.
  • Menstrual cups and discs. A 2019 Lancet Public Health review says menstrual cups are made of medical-grade silicone, rubber, latex, or elastomer. None of those are metal.
  • Pads and liners. A pad sits in your underwear. If the center asks you to change into a gown, ask whether you can keep plain cotton underwear and a pad on, or whether they supply disposable underwear.

One practical point: an MRI visit can take a while once you add check-in, screening, and changing. The FDA says to change a tampon every 4 to 8 hours and never wear one longer than 8 hours. Put in a fresh one shortly before you leave, or bring a spare to change at the center.

If you use an IUD or an arm implant for birth control, that is a separate screening question. We cover it in MRI with an IUD or birth control implant.

Is period underwear a problem in an MRI?

It can be. Period underwear is often sold as "antimicrobial" or "odor control." Some fabric treatments of this kind use silver or other metals. You usually cannot see them, and a hand-held metal detector may not find them.

This is not a theory. A 2013 report in the American Journal of Neuroradiology describes a thermal burn during an MRI. It was likely caused by invisible silver-embedded microfibers in an ordinary-looking undershirt. The authors warned that hidden metal fibers are increasingly common in athletic and "tech" clothing. They stressed that patients should change into facility-provided garments.

RadiologyInfo gives the same warning. Its safety page says clothing may contain "metallic threads or metal-based antibacterial compounds" that can heat up and burn the patient. Its body MRI page adds that some clothing contains metallic fibers that cannot be worn inside the MRI.

We could not confirm which period underwear brands use silver, because labels vary and change. The safe move is simple. On scan day, wear a tampon, cup, or pad with plain cotton underwear. If you only use period underwear, bring a backup and ask the technologist before you change.

Does your cycle matter for a breast MRI?

Sometimes. In women who still have periods, normal breast tissue takes up MRI contrast dye in a pattern that changes across the cycle. Radiologists call this background parenchymal enhancement. When it is strong, it can make the images harder to read.

A common claim is that breast MRI should be done on days 7 to 14 of the cycle. The source guideline says something slightly different. The 2008 European Society of Breast Imaging (EUSOBI) guideline states that the best time is between the 5th and 12th day after the start of the menstrual cycle. Day 1 is the first day of bleeding.

Newer evidence questions how much timing matters for screening. A 2019 study in the Journal of Breast Imaging looked at 320 screening breast MRIs in 244 premenopausal women at high risk. Background enhancement and diagnostic accuracy did not differ by cycle phase or week. The authors concluded that timing the exam to a specific week does not reliably improve the scan.

What this means for you: some centers still schedule breast MRI by cycle day, and others do not. If your center gives you a window, follow it. If your scan is urgent, such as after a new cancer diagnosis, ask your doctor whether cycle timing applies at all. You can read more about who needs this test in breast MRI: who needs one, and about screening in general on mammogram.md.

Does your period affect a pelvic MRI?

Usually not enough to change your appointment. A period can change how the uterus looks on MRI, but the expert groups do not ask you to avoid it.

The 2017 European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR) endometriosis guideline reviewed the evidence on timing. One study it cites found the inner layer of the uterus can look falsely thick during menstruation, which can mimic adenomyosis. Other studies found no difference in disease extent between scans done during and outside a period. The panel made no recommendation on timing.

The 2025 ESUR consensus update went further. It states that no specific timing in the menstrual cycle is recommended for endometriosis MRI. 19 of 20 experts agreed. The same update lists vaginal gel (about 10 mL) as an option, not a routine step. If your pelvic protocol uses vaginal gel, ask whether to remove your tampon or cup first.

What should you do on scan day if you have your period?

Use this checklist, built from the FDA, ACR/RSNA, and ESUR guidance above:

  1. Check your prep sheet for a required cycle day. This mainly applies to breast MRI.
  2. Put in a fresh tampon or empty your cup before you leave. Bring a spare tampon or pad.
  3. Wear plain cotton underwear. Leave period underwear, athletic wear, and anything labeled antimicrobial at home, or bring it in a bag.
  4. Expect to change into a gown. Ask whether you can keep underwear and a pad on.
  5. Tell the technologist you have your period and what product you are using. For a pelvic scan, ask whether they want a tampon or cup removed.
  6. Mention a late or missed period. Staff ask about pregnancy during screening, and it matters most for contrast. See MRI while pregnant.
  7. Tell staff about any IUD, implant, or medicated skin patch, even if you think it is not metal.

Telling the technologist is routine. It helps them plan the exam and offer you what you need.

The bottom line

You can have an MRI on your period. Tampons, menstrual cups, and pads have no metal parts, so most people can keep them in. The thing to avoid is clothing with hidden metal fibers, including some antimicrobial period underwear. Cycle timing matters only for some breast MRIs, and expert groups no longer ask for it for endometriosis MRI. When in doubt, tell the technologist and follow your center's prep sheet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will the MRI magnet pull on a tampon or menstrual cup?

No. The FDA says tampons are made of cotton, rayon, or a blend. A 2019 Lancet Public Health review says menstrual cups are made of silicone, rubber, latex, or elastomer. None of these are magnetic. The magnet only acts on certain metals.

Do I have to tell the technologist I am on my period?

You do not have to, but it helps. Mention it if you are having a pelvic or breast scan, because it can affect timing or whether a tampon or cup should come out. It also lets staff offer you a pad or underwear if you need to change.

Can heavy bleeding or cramps affect my MRI?

Bleeding itself does not affect safety. Cramps can make it hard to lie still, and movement blurs images. If cramps are bad, ask your doctor what you can take beforehand and tell the technologist so they can make you comfortable.

Should I reschedule a pelvic MRI because my period started?

Usually not. The 2025 ESUR consensus says no specific timing in the menstrual cycle is recommended for endometriosis MRI. Call the center if your doctor asked for a certain cycle day, and follow their advice.

Is it safe to wear period underwear under the hospital gown?

Not if it has silver or other metal treatments, which you often cannot see. A 2013 case report linked a burn to invisible silver microfibers in an undershirt. Wear plain cotton underwear with a tampon, cup, or pad instead.
