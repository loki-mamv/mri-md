Can You Have an MRI if You Might Have Metal in Your Eye?

November 14, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Often yes, but only after screening. Per the American College of Radiology's 2019 MR safety guidance, anyone who saw a doctor for a metal injury to the eye should get one or two orbital X-rays before an MRI. A clear X-ray usually means the scan goes ahead. If metal shows up, the MRI is postponed pending review.

Why is metal in the eye a problem for MRI?

An MRI scanner uses a very strong magnet that is always on. The FDA notes that this field attracts magnetic objects, so careful screening of people and objects is critical. A tiny sliver of iron or steel in the eye is a magnetic object.

The eye has no thick tissue to hold a fragment in place. If a ferromagnetic fragment moves or twists in the field, it can tear delicate structures inside the eye. A 1986 case report in the American Journal of Neuroradiology described unilateral blindness after an MRI, caused by an iron-containing fragment inside the eye. That case is one reason eye screening became routine.

The risk is real but rare. Most people who worked with metal have nothing in their eyes. Screening exists to find the few who do, before they enter the scanner room.

Who is at risk of having a metal fragment in the eye?

A fragment usually gets into the eye at high speed. You may feel a sting and forget about it. The groups that screeners ask about most are:

  • Grinders and machinists. Angle grinders, lathes, and drills throw off hot steel particles.
  • Welders and metal fabricators. Chipping slag and grinding welds both spray small fragments.
  • People who hammer metal on metal. A chisel or nail can chip and send a sliver into the eye.
  • People with blast or shrapnel injuries. This includes military service and explosions.
  • Anyone with a past eye injury from metal, even if a doctor treated it years ago.

Not knowing about an injury does not rule one out. A 2018 case report described a 65-year-old metalworker with no known history of eye trauma. He felt intense eye pain during a brain MRI, and an X-ray then confirmed a retained metal fragment.

A job title alone is not proof of risk, though. A 2000 cost analysis in AJNR concluded that occupational history by itself is not enough to require an X-ray. The key question is whether you had an eye injury and whether it was fully treated.

How does the MRI screening form check for eye metal?

Every MRI center gives you a written safety questionnaire before the scan. It almost always asks whether you have ever had metal in your eye, worked with metal, or had an eye injury. A technologist then reviews your answers with you in person.

The follow-up questions matter more than the checkbox. NHS Scotland's MRI physics guidance describes a typical sequence:

  1. Did you ever have an eye injury involving metal?
  2. If yes, did a doctor examine your eye at the time?
  3. Did the doctor tell you the eye was normal, or that the metal was removed completely?

Under that guidance, a clear "yes, it was all removed" lets the scan go ahead. If metal was left in, or you never had a follow-up eye exam, the MRI is postponed until an X-ray is done. The same guidance lets the radiographer order an X-ray anyway if your history seems unclear. It also suggests talking privately with teenagers, who may not answer honestly in front of a parent.

The 2000 AJNR analysis boiled this down to one question: "Did a doctor get it all out?" One more point catches many patients by surprise. The ACR's 2019 guidance states that a past uneventful MRI is not proof that the next one is safe after a metal eye injury.

Orbital X-ray or CT: which screening test is better?

The standard screening test is a plain X-ray of the orbits (the eye sockets). The ACR guidance now recommends one or two views for patients with an eye injury, because no data show that two views are safer than one. The X-ray takes a few minutes and uses a small radiation dose.

X-rays miss small fragments. In a 2017 phantom study in Radiography, observers found 72.1% of planted metal fragments overall. Detection was 93% for large fragments, 76% for medium, and only 46% for small ones.

CT sees much smaller pieces. In a 1997 Ophthalmology study, CT found steel fragments larger than 0.06 cubic millimeters every time. For smaller fragments, detection fell to 45% to 65%. A 2001 study in pig eyes found that spiral CT detected every 0.5 mm metal fragment. For this reason, CT is the usual test after a recent or serious eye injury. Our MRI vs CT guide explains how the two scanners differ.

Both tests have the same limit. They show that something dense is there, but not whether it is magnetic. Neither can promise that no fragment exists. An earlier MRI can help in one way: the ACR notes that a radiologist can study its image distortion around the eyes for clues.

Screening also turns up false alarms. A 2024 study of 47,237 pre-MRI orbital X-rays found that 328 (0.69%) were first read as positive. On expert re-review, only 39 (0.083%) showed a fragment in a risky or uncertain spot. Almost all of the false positives had been denied their MRI.

What happens if a metal fragment is found?

A positive X-ray stops the MRI for that day. It does not mean you can never have one. A radiologist reviews the images, and the next step depends on the details:

  • Uncertain finding: a CT of the orbits may show whether the spot is a real fragment, and where it sits.
  • Confirmed fragment in or near the eye: the team usually picks another test, such as CT or ultrasound, if it can answer the medical question.
  • MRI still needed: you may be sent to an eye doctor first. The radiologist and your doctor then weigh the risk against the benefit.

If you are told a fragment is present, ask for a copy of the report. Bring it to every future imaging visit so you do not repeat the process from the start.

What should you do before your MRI if you might have eye metal?

If you cannot remember whether metal ever got in your eye, say so. "I don't know" is a safe answer, and staff will usually order an X-ray. Use this checklist in the days before your scan:

  1. Think back. List jobs, hobbies, or military service that involved grinding, welding, cutting, or hammering metal.
  2. Recall any eye injury. Note the year, what happened, and whether a doctor or clinic treated it.
  3. Find old records. Look for an eye doctor's note or ER discharge paper that says the metal was removed.
  4. Call the imaging center ahead. Tell them about your history so they can book an orbital X-ray before the MRI, not on the day.
  5. Answer the form honestly. Tick "yes" or "unsure" rather than "no" when in doubt.
  6. Bring past imaging reports. A clear orbital X-ray or head CT done after the injury may help the radiologist.
  7. Speak up in the scanner. If you feel eye pain, pressure, or pulling, use the call button at once.

For the other items on the screening form, such as implants, see our MRI safety guide. If your MRI is for breast screening and it is delayed, the options at mammogram.md may help in the meantime.

The bottom line

Possible metal in the eye rarely stops an MRI for good. It does call for a careful screening history and, often, a quick orbital X-ray. Tell the staff about any metal work or eye injury, even an old one. Let the radiologist decide whether a CT or another test is needed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do all metalworkers need an orbital X-ray before MRI?

Not always. Many centers ask whether you had an eye injury and whether a doctor removed all the metal. If metal was left in, or you never had the eye checked, an X-ray is usually done first. Local policies vary, so ask your imaging center.

I had an MRI before without problems. Do I still need screening?

Yes, if you have a history of a metal eye injury. The American College of Radiology's 2019 guidance states that a past uneventful MRI is not proof that the next one is safe. A radiologist may still review the old images for useful clues.

How long does an orbital X-ray take?

The X-ray itself takes only a few minutes. A radiologist must read it before the MRI can start, which can add time. Booking the X-ray before your MRI day avoids delays.

What if I feel pain in my eye during the MRI?

Press the call button right away and tell the technologist. Eye pain, pressure, or a pulling feeling can be a sign of a hidden metal fragment. The team can stop the scan and move you out of the magnet.

Can a CT scan replace an MRI if I have a metal fragment in my eye?

Sometimes. CT is safe with metal fragments and answers many medical questions, such as bleeding or fractures. For other questions, an MRI gives better detail, and your doctor and radiologist will weigh the options.
