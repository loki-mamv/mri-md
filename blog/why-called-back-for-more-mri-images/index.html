Why Would You Be Called Back for More MRI Images?

The short answer: A callback for more MRI images usually means the radiologist wants a clearer look, not that something is wrong. Common reasons are an unclear spot, a need for contrast, or a planned follow-up scan. Brain MRI finds an incidental tumor-like spot in about 0.7% of people with no symptoms, per a 2009 BMJ meta-analysis.

What does it mean when you are called back after an MRI?

A callback means the radiologist read your images and wants more information before giving a final answer. It can happen days after the scan. It is different from a rescan during the visit, when the technologist repeats a blurry series on the spot. That second case is covered in our guide to what happens if you move during an MRI.

Radiologists ask for more imaging often. A 2009 Radiology study of 5.9 million exams at one academic hospital found that reports recommending more imaging rose from about 6% to 12% between 1995 and 2008. That study counted all imaging types, not only MRI. It also found that recommendations were about five times more likely when an exam had a positive finding.

A callback does not mean the radiologist has found cancer. Most often it means one spot on the images cannot be labeled with confidence yet.

Why would the radiologist want more pictures?

The reasons fall into a few groups:

  • An incidental finding. This is a spot found by chance, in an area the scan was not ordered to check. The American College of Radiology (ACR) notes that incidental findings have risen with wider use of CT and MRI. It also says many are unlikely to pose a health risk.
  • An indeterminate finding. The spot is real, but the first images cannot show what it is. Extra sequences, such as fat-sensitive or contrast-enhanced images, can often answer the question.
  • A need for contrast. Your first scan may have been done without contrast dye. If a spot needs to be checked for blood flow, the radiologist may ask for a second scan with contrast. Our MRI with contrast guide explains how that works.
  • A planned follow-up. Some findings are very likely benign but are worth a second look months later to confirm they have not changed.
  • A technical or protocol issue. A sequence may have been missed, cut short, or set up for the wrong question. A metal implant or movement may also have blurred one area.

How do radiologists decide which findings need more imaging?

For many organs, radiologists follow published decision guides. The ACR Incidental Findings Committee has written white papers for common incidental spots seen on CT or MRI. These include adrenal masses (2017) and pancreatic cysts (2017). Each one uses a flowchart based on patient factors and imaging features. Each flowchart ends in one of two outcomes: the spot is benign enough to stop follow-up, or there is a next step.

Some organs have their own scoring systems. Many of them include a "probably benign, check again later" category:

  • Kidney cysts: The Bosniak classification, version 2019 sorts cystic kidney masses by cancer risk. The 2019 update added formal MRI criteria for the first time.
  • Liver spots in people at risk for liver cancer: The ACR LI-RADS system gives CT and MRI liver findings a category. Lower categories can lead to repeat imaging instead of a biopsy.
  • Breast MRI: A BI-RADS 3 result means "probably benign." It usually leads to a short-interval follow-up MRI, often at 6 months.

The breast numbers show why this approach exists. In a 2020 AJR study of 6,672 high-risk screening breast MRIs, 3% were rated BI-RADS 3. Of those, 6% turned out to be cancer, and 12 of 13 cancers were stage 0 or I. So most BI-RADS 3 spots were benign, and the follow-up found most cancers early. Callbacks after a screening mammogram work in a similar way, and mammogram.md covers that process.

Does a callback mean something is wrong?

Usually not. Here is what a callback does and does not mean:

  • It does mean the radiologist could not give a final answer for at least one area.
  • It does mean a guideline or the radiologist's judgment calls for another look.
  • It does not mean you have cancer or that the finding is dangerous.
  • It does not mean your first scan was wasted. The new images are compared against it.

Age matters. The BMJ brain MRI meta-analysis found that incidental tumor-like findings became more common with age. It also found more findings with high-resolution sequences than with standard ones (4.3% vs 1.7%). Sharper pictures show more small things, and most of them are harmless.

What happens at the follow-up scan?

A follow-up scan is often shorter than the first one. The team may only scan one body area, or add the missing sequences. A full MRI exam usually lasts about 30 to 60 minutes, per MedlinePlus, and a targeted add-on can take less time. If contrast is added, you will get an IV line, usually in your hand or forearm.

The radiologist will compare the new images with the old ones. For a planned interval scan, the key question is often whether the spot has grown or changed. A stable spot over time is reassuring. Our guide on how long MRI results take explains what to expect after this second scan.

Sometimes the next test is not an MRI. Depending on the question, the doctor may order an ultrasound, CT, blood test, or biopsy. The ACR Appropriateness Criteria help doctors choose the right imaging test for a given clinical problem.

What should you ask when you are called back?

Follow-up recommendations are easy to lose track of. A 2021 study in the Journal of the American College of Radiology looked at CT reports that recommended more imaging for incidental findings. Only 39.1% of those recommendations were completed. Use this checklist so yours is not missed:

  1. What did the radiologist see, and in which part of the body?
  2. Is this an incidental finding, or is it related to why I had the scan?
  3. What is the next test: more MRI sequences, a contrast scan, or a different test?
  4. Will I need contrast? If I have kidney disease, is that a problem?
  5. Is this urgent, or is it a planned follow-up in weeks or months?
  6. Which guideline or category applies (for example, BI-RADS, LI-RADS, or Bosniak)?
  7. Who will order the follow-up scan, and who will call me with the results?
  8. Will my insurance need a new prior authorization?
  9. Can the new scan be done at the same center so the images can be compared?
  10. Can I get a copy of the report and the images?

The bottom line

Being called back for more MRI images is common, and it most often means a spot needs a clearer look. The reason may be an incidental or unclear finding, a need for contrast, a planned follow-up, or a technical gap. Published guides from the ACR and other groups shape most of these decisions. Ask what the finding is, what the next test is, and who owns the follow-up. Then make sure the scan gets done.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it bad if the MRI center calls me to come back?

Not necessarily. Most callbacks are for a spot that needs a clearer look, a contrast scan, or a missing sequence. Ask what the radiologist saw and how urgent the next step is.

Why didn't they just use contrast the first time?

Many MRI exams do not need contrast, so doctors often start without it. If a spot needs to be checked for blood flow, a contrast scan is added later. This avoids giving contrast to people who do not need it.

How long after an MRI would they call me back?

It varies. A missing or blurry sequence is often found within days, once the radiologist reads the scan. A planned interval follow-up, such as a 6-month breast MRI for a BI-RADS 3 result, is scheduled months ahead.

Do I have to pay again for the follow-up MRI?

Often yes, because it is usually billed as a new exam. Your insurer may also need a new prior authorization. If the repeat is only for a technical problem at the center, ask whether they will cover it.

Can I skip a recommended follow-up scan?

That is a choice to make with your doctor, not alone. Studies show many recommended follow-ups are never completed, and some findings do need tracking. Ask what the risk is of waiting or skipping.
