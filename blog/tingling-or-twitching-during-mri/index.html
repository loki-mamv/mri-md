Is Tingling or Muscle Twitching During an MRI Normal?

November 12, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Usually, yes. The scanner's fast-switching gradient coils can briefly stimulate nerves. The ACR Manual on MR Safety (2024 edition) says the feeling "ranges from tingling to muscle contractions" and generally poses minimal danger. Burning, heat in one spot, or real pain is different. Press the call button for those.

What does nerve stimulation in an MRI feel like?

The medical name is peripheral nerve stimulation, or PNS. Most people who notice it describe a light tingle, a tapping, or a small twitch under the skin. It comes and goes with the scan, and it stops when the scanner goes quiet.

The FDA's MRI benefits and risks page says the pulsed gradient fields "may also cause peripheral muscle or nerve stimulation that may feel like a twitching sensation." A classic review in the Journal of Magnetic Resonance Imaging describes perceptible PNS as "tingling or tapping sensations" (Schaefer et al., 2000).

In a 2019 volunteer study of a high-performance head gradient coil, the most common words people used were "tingling" and "vibration" (Tan et al., Magnetic Resonance in Medicine). Only one volunteer called a stimulus painful, and that person rated the pain as mild. Where you feel it depends on the coil and the scan. In that study, most sensations were in the face, upper torso, and neck.

Why does the scanner make nerves fire?

An MRI uses three fields. The main magnet is always on. Radio wave pulses are the source of warmth. The third field comes from gradient coils, which switch on and off very fast to encode where each signal comes from.

A magnetic field that changes quickly induces a small electric current in nearby tissue. The rate of change is written as dB/dt, in tesla per second. When the induced current is strong enough, it can trigger a nerve or muscle cell, just as a light touch would. The ACR manual states that "nerve and muscle cells can be stimulated by currents induced by the time-varying gradient magnetic field."

The same rapid switching makes the loud knocking and buzzing. The ACR manual names the gradients as the source of both the acoustic noise and PNS. So if the twitching seems to keep time with the loudest sounds, that is expected. Our guide on why an MRI machine is so loud explains the noise side.

Which scans are most likely to cause it?

The ACR manual says the strength of stimulation "is a function of the pulse characteristics and repetition rate." Fast scans that switch the gradients hard and often are the usual triggers.

The manual lists echo-planar imaging (often used for diffusion-weighted imaging), functional MRI, perfusion imaging, and MR angiography as sequences that "require a rapid variation of the gradient magnetic fields." Diffusion scans are common in brain, prostate, and cancer protocols, so many patients meet them.

Gradient fields from more than one direction add together. The Schaefer review notes that fields from simultaneous gradient axes "combine almost as a vector sum to produce stimulation." That is one reason the same person may feel nothing on one series and a tingle on the next.

How do safety limits keep it in check?

PNS is a known, regulated effect. Scanner makers design to the international standard IEC 60601-2-33. The ACR manual summarizes its gradient limits this way:

  • Normal Operating Mode: the gradients may not exceed 80% of the mean threshold for PNS. The threshold is defined as the onset of sensation.
  • First Level Controlled Operating Mode: the gradients may not exceed 100% of the mean threshold. This mode needs medical supervision, and the operator must confirm it on the console.
  • Second Level Controlled Operating Mode: reserved for approved research protocols.

The limit is set against a mean (average) threshold. People with a lower-than-average threshold can still feel a light tingle within the limits. That is why the feeling is common and not a sign of a fault.

The margin to discomfort is wide. The Schaefer review reports that patients "may become uncomfortable at amplitudes 50%-100% above perception thresholds." In dogs, heart stimulation needed nearly 80 times the energy that nerve stimulation needed.

The FDA draws its own line. Its guidance on significant risk MRI studies (2014) treats any gradient rate of change "sufficient to produce severe discomfort or painful nerve stimulation" as significant risk. That level is outside routine clinical scanning.

How is it different from heating or a burn?

Tingling and twitching come from the gradients. Heat comes from the radio wave pulses, which the scanner limits by specific absorption rate. The two can feel alike at first, so pay attention to the pattern.

  • Nerve stimulation: a pins-and-needles or tapping feeling, or a small muscle jump. It starts and stops with the noise. It often affects a broad area, such as the back, sides, or face. It leaves no mark.
  • Heating: slow, even warmth that builds over minutes. This is usually normal. Our article on why you feel warm during an MRI covers it.
  • Warning signs: heat or burning in one small spot, a sting at a tattoo, a patch, or a skin-to-skin contact point, or pain that keeps building. These need the call button now.

If you have an implanted wire or lead, the rules are stricter. The ACR manual says leads near the heart, brain, or spinal cord carry higher risk with fast sequences. Report any new feeling near a device right away.

What should you do if you feel tingling or twitching?

Use this checklist, built from the FDA and ACR guidance above.

  1. Before the scan, tell the technologist about any implant, lead, stimulator, or past nerve problem. Ask if they expect long diffusion or fast sequences.
  2. Get set up well. Keep your hands at your sides, not clasped. Do not cross your legs or arms. This setup is standard to avoid burn loops, and it keeps you comfortable.
  3. If you feel a light tingle or twitch that keeps time with the noise, stay still. Moving can blur the images and force a repeat. See what happens if you move during an MRI.
  4. Tell the technologist at the next pause where you felt it and how strong it was. They can often adjust the sequence or stay in Normal Operating Mode.
  5. Press the call button now if the feeling turns painful, if a muscle jerks hard enough to move you, or if you feel burning or heat in one spot.
  6. After the scan, the sensation should be gone. Numbness, weakness, or tingling that lasts is not typical PNS. Report it to your doctor.

If you are trying to sort out tingling that started apart from any scan, a symptom guide such as symptom.md can help you prepare questions for your doctor.

The bottom line

Brief tingling, tapping, or twitching during an MRI is a known effect of the fast-switching gradient fields. The FDA and the ACR both describe it, and the IEC standard keeps routine scans below the average threshold of sensation. It is most common during fast sequences such as diffusion and echo-planar imaging. Mention it at the next pause. Press the call button for pain, a strong jerk, or any heat or burning in one spot.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can MRI nerve stimulation damage my nerves?

The ACR Manual on MR Safety says nerve stimulation from MRI generally presents minimal discomfort or danger. Scanners keep routine scans below the average threshold of sensation. Tingling or numbness that lasts after the scan is not typical, so report it to your doctor.

Why did I feel twitching on one MRI but not on another?

The effect depends on the scan sequence, the scanner's gradient system, and your own threshold. Fast sequences such as diffusion-weighted and echo-planar imaging switch the gradients harder. Your body position and the area scanned also change where and how strongly you feel it.

Is it safe to keep still through the tingling?

For a light tingle that keeps time with the noise, yes. Staying still protects image quality. Tell the technologist at the next pause, and press the call button at once for pain, a strong jerk, or burning.

Can the technologist reduce MRI twitching?

Often, yes. The ACR manual says operators can reduce the chance of nerve stimulation by using Normal Operating Mode for the gradients. The technologist can also change some sequence settings or the order of the scans.

Does twitching mean the MRI machine is broken?

No. Nerve stimulation is a known effect that the FDA lists for the gradient fields. The safety limits are set against an average threshold, so people with a lower threshold can feel it on a normal working scanner.
