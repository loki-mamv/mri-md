How Soon After Surgery Can You Have an MRI?

November 19, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Most people can have an MRI right after surgery. Frank Shellock's 2021 post-operative guidelines say passive, nonmagnetic implants can be scanned immediately at 3 tesla or less, unless the device label states a wait. A few magnetic coils, stents or filters may need six weeks. Your surgeon may still wait for swelling to settle.

Is there a required waiting period for an MRI after surgery?

For most operations, no. The timing question has two separate parts. The first is safety: can the metal left in your body move or heat up in the scanner? The second is usefulness: will the pictures show anything your doctor can act on while the area is still healing?

The safety part is usually settled fast. Shellock, an MRI safety researcher at the University of Southern California, wrote that a "passive implant" made from nonferromagnetic material can be scanned immediately after placement on a scanner of 3 tesla or less. A passive implant has no battery or electronic part. Clips, staples, sutures, most stents, screws and plates fall in this group. His guideline also states that if the instructions for use or MRI labeling do not list a wait, there is no need to delay the exam.

The usefulness part depends on the surgery and on what your doctor wants to see. That is often the real reason for a delay.

Which surgical implants can be scanned right away?

The Shellock post-operative guideline sorts implants into three groups by how they react to a magnet:

  • Nonmagnetic passive implants: MRI can happen immediately at 3 tesla or less. The guideline notes that doctors have even placed stents, coils and filters during MRI-guided procedures in 1.5 and 3 tesla scanners.
  • Weakly magnetic implants that are rigidly fixed (for example orthopedic screws, plates and rods anchored in bone): MRI can also happen immediately after the operation.
  • Implants that are clearly magnetic (certain coils, stents and filters labeled "magnetic"): a wait of six weeks may be needed, so that tissue grows in and holds the device in place.

The same guideline adds a caution. These rules cover pulling and twisting forces only. Heating from the radio waves needs its own check. If the implant cannot be identified, or the tissue holding it may not be secure, the patient should not be scanned. Joint replacements follow the same logic, as our guide to MRI with a hip or knee replacement explains.

Do you have to wait six weeks after a heart stent?

Not for coronary stents, according to current guidance. Many patients still hear about a six to eight week wait. That advice came from older package inserts. A 2003 Mayo Clinic study in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology noted that manufacturers then recommended waiting eight weeks after coronary stent placement.

That study reviewed 111 patients who had a 1.5 tesla MRI less than 8 weeks after a coronary stent. The median time from stent to scan was 18 days, and the range was 0 to 54 days. No stent thrombosis (clotting inside the stent) occurred. The authors concluded that postponing MRI did not appear necessary.

Shellock's coronary stent guidelines go further. They state that the old belief in a wait of six weeks or longer "has been refuted," because no known coronary stents are made of ferromagnetic metal. They also state that patients with any commercially available coronary stent can be scanned immediately at 1.5 or 3 tesla, under set conditions. These include the scanner's normal operating mode and a limit of 15 minutes per sequence. Shellock reports that no adverse event has ever been reported with MRI in these patients.

That stent guidance does not apply to peripheral vascular stents, aortic stent grafts, biliary, ureteral, tracheal or esophageal stents. For those, the MRI team follows the device label. Pacemakers and defibrillators are a separate topic, covered in our guide to MRI with a pacemaker or defibrillator.

Which surgical devices need extra checks before an MRI?

A few items get closer review no matter how long ago the surgery was. The 2024 ACR Manual on MR Safety names them:

  • Aneurysm clips. The manual lists them among passive devices for which the exact make, model and material must be identified before scanning.
  • Some endoscopic clips. The manual warns that certain clips used in endoscopic procedures are MR Unsafe. These clips are often placed to stop bleeding after a polyp is removed. If you had a recent polyp removal, tell the MRI team. Our sister site colonoscopy.md explains what happens during that procedure.
  • Skin staples and metal sutures close together. Each one carries a small heating risk. Items that sit close to or touch each other may raise the risk of a burn. The manual advises patients to report warmth or burning right away, and cold packs may help.

The manual also lets each MRI site treat some groups, such as coronary stents and orthopedic hardware, as relatively low risk under its written procedures. Those sites may not need the exact model of each stent or screw.

Why might your surgeon wait for swelling to settle first?

Fresh surgery changes the tissue. Swelling, fluid, blood and early scar can look like the problem the surgeon just fixed. Two examples show the range.

Lower back surgery. A 1995 Swedish study scanned 20 patients who did well after lumbar discectomy. Scans were done before surgery and at 5 days, 6 weeks and 4 months after it. Deformation of the spinal sac appeared in 13 patients before surgery, 19 at 5 days, 15 at 6 weeks and 12 at 4 months. The authors linked these findings to swelling and scar, and urged careful reading of early scans. A 1997 Italian study of 30 patients found a disc-like "pseudohernia" in 80% at 3 days after surgery. Half of those still showed it at 8 weeks, though the early images did not match how patients felt.

Brain tumor surgery. Here the timing runs the other way. A 1994 study of 60 patients after high-grade glioma surgery found that contrast MRI in days 1 to 3 avoided surgery-related enhancement. That made leftover tumor easier to measure. A 2019 review notes that a 72-hour window became the traditional advice, and that newer studies now question it.

So your surgeon may order a scan within days, or ask you to wait weeks or months. The choice depends on the question the scan must answer, not only on the metal.

What should you bring, and who decides when it is safe?

The MRI team makes the safety call, not the patient. The ACR manual says the implant must be identified and checked against its MR Safe, MR Conditional or MR Unsafe status. It names operative notes, device identification cards and the medical record as sources. That identification should be documented in writing or electronically.

The FDA's October 2023 guidance on MR labeling recommends that implant makers supply a patient medical device card. The card should name the device, give its MRI safety status, and list the scan conditions or tell staff where to find them. Use this checklist before a post-surgery scan:

  1. Ask your surgeon's office for the implant card, or the device name and model from the operative note.
  2. Write down the date of surgery and every procedure, including endoscopies and stent placements.
  3. Tell the scheduler about skin staples, drains, wires or anything still on the skin.
  4. Ask your surgeon whether the scan should wait for healing, and why.
  5. During the scan, use the call ball at once if you feel warmth or burning. Our guide on feeling warm during an MRI explains normal warmth.

The bottom line

Metal from most operations does not force a wait. Nonmagnetic implants and bone-fixed hardware can be scanned right away at 3 tesla or less, and coronary stents can be scanned immediately under set conditions. A wait applies mainly to clearly magnetic devices or labels that state one. The more common reason to wait is that swelling and fresh scar can blur the answer. Bring your implant card and let the MRI team and your surgeon set the date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I have an MRI with surgical staples still in my skin?

Often yes. The 2024 ACR Manual on MR Safety says the heating risk from a single skin staple or metal suture is quite small. Staples that sit close together may raise the burn risk, so tell the technologist and report any warmth at once.

Will titanium screws or plates show up on my MRI?

They usually cause some distortion on the images near the metal, but they are rarely a safety barrier. Shellock's guideline says weakly magnetic implants fixed in bone can be scanned immediately after surgery. The radiologist may adjust the scan to reduce the distortion.

Can an MRI loosen dissolvable stitches or internal sutures?

Absorbable sutures are not metal and do not react to the magnet. Metal sutures and clips used inside the body are passive implants, and most can be scanned at once under current guidance. Your MRI team checks any metal from the operative note.

I had a stent last week and the hospital wants to wait. Is that wrong?

Not necessarily. Coronary stent guidance supports scanning right away, but other stents, such as biliary or peripheral stents, follow their own labels. The team may also wait for clinical reasons, so ask them what the wait is for.

What if I do not have my implant card?

Ask the hospital where you had surgery for the operative note, which lists the device name and model. The ACR manual accepts operative notes and the medical record as sources. Without a clear identification, some devices cannot be scanned.
