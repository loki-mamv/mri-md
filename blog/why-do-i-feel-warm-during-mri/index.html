Why Do I Feel Warm During an MRI?

November 10, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: An MRI sends radio wave pulses into your body, and your tissue absorbs some of that energy as heat. The FDA states that this energy "could lead to heating of the body," and that the chance of heating is greater in long exams. Mild, even warmth is normal. For a hot spot, burning, or tingling, press the call button.

What makes your body warm up in an MRI?

An MRI uses three things: a strong static magnet, gradient coils that switch on and off, and radiofrequency (RF) pulses. The magnet itself does not heat you. The RF pulses do. They tip the hydrogen atoms in your tissue so the scanner can read their signal.

Some of that RF energy turns into heat in your tissue. The FDA's MRI benefits and risks page lists this directly: the RF energy "could lead to heating of the body," and "the potential for heating is greater during long MRI examinations."

Your body gets rid of this heat the normal way, through blood flow and sweat. That is why most people feel, at most, a gentle warmth that builds over the exam and fades soon after.

What is specific absorption rate (SAR)?

Specific absorption rate, or SAR, is the measure of how much RF energy your body absorbs. It is given in watts per kilogram of body weight (W/kg). The scanner tracks SAR for each sequence and keeps it under set limits.

The international standard for MRI scanners, IEC 60601-2-33, sets operating modes. In the lowest one, called Normal Operating Mode, FDA labeling examples use a whole-body SAR of 2 W/kg and a head SAR of 3.2 W/kg, per the FDA guidance on testing and labeling devices for the MR environment (2023). The same guidance notes that whole-body SAR "can exceed 2 W/kg for short intervals" in that mode.

A separate FDA guidance on significant risk MRI studies (2014) sets the line where a research scan becomes significant risk. That line is a whole-body SAR above 4 W/kg averaged over 15 minutes, or a head SAR above 3.2 W/kg averaged over 10 minutes.

The standard keeps changing. The British Institute of Radiology summary of the 2022 edition reports that an older hard stop on total absorbed energy was removed. Scanners still show a warning when that energy level is reached.

Why can a 3T scanner feel warmer than 1.5T?

A 3T magnet needs a higher RF frequency than a 1.5T magnet. The energy deposited rises steeply with that frequency. A 2016 study in a Japanese radiologic technology journal states that SAR at 3T is "approximately four times as much as the 1.5 T" for the same scan.

The SAR limits do not get looser at 3T. The FDA significant risk guidance gives one set of SAR numbers, with no separate figure for 3T. So the scanner and technologist change the sequences to stay under the limit. In practice, a 3T exam can run closer to that limit, and some people notice more warmth.

The same 2016 study tested artificial hip joints in a body-like gel. Temperature rise had "a good linear relationship" with SAR, which is why reducing SAR lowers heating near metal. Our guide to 1.5T vs 3T MRI covers the image quality trade-offs.

When is warmth a warning sign?

Even, all-over warmth is expected. Heat in one small spot is different. It means energy is concentrating there, and that is how burns start.

The FDA receives around 300 adverse event reports for MRI scanners and coils each year, and most describe heating or burns, per the FDA. Second-degree burns are the most commonly reported patient problem. The FDA also calls adverse events from MRI "very rare," given that millions of scans happen in the US each year.

An FDA 10-year review in Medical Physics (Delfino et al., 2019) studied 1,548 reports from 2008 to 2017. Thermal events were 59% of them. The authors grouped the likely causes:

  • Unclear cause: 39% of thermal reports.
  • Contact with an object in the bore: 28%. MRI coils and their cables were 54% of these, and ECG electrodes or leads were 15%.
  • Skin-to-skin contact: 16%. Examples were inner thighs, calves, and hands of patients with crossed arms. These contacts can form a loop that carries current.
  • Touching the bore wall: 10%, most often at the arms, elbows, and shoulders.

The same review found that the majority of these events had known causes and were preventable.

What about tattoos, metal, and patches?

Anything that conducts electricity can focus RF energy into one spot. In the FDA review, the objects tied to burns included tattoos and permanent makeup (6 reports), dermal patches (3), pulse oximeters (7), and orthopedic implants (8). Patient clothing was linked to 12 burns, and the authors named silver-impregnated clothing as one source.

Tattoo reactions have their own causes and risk factors. Our article on getting an MRI with tattoos explains them, so this page does not repeat them. Implants are a separate topic too. The FDA notes that RF energy may heat an implant and the tissue around it, which is why each implant has its own scan conditions. The MRI safety guide covers screening.

What should you tell the technologist?

You hold a call button, often a rubber squeeze ball, during the whole scan. The FDA's patient page suggests you ask, "Where is the call button I can use to let you know if there is a problem?" Use this checklist, built from the FDA review findings:

  1. Before you go in: say if you have a tattoo, a medicine patch, an implant, or clothing with metallic thread. Change into the gown if you are asked to.
  2. On the table: keep your hands at your sides, not clasped. Do not cross your ankles or let bare thighs touch. Ask for a pad if your arms or body touch the bore wall.
  3. If a cable touches your skin: tell the technologist. The review found burns when only a sheet or blanket separated a coil cable from the patient.
  4. During the scan: squeeze the ball right away for any hot spot, burning, stinging, or tingling. The technologist can stop the sequence.
  5. After the scan: look at any spot that felt hot. Show red or blistered skin to the staff before you leave.

A feeling of heat in a small space can also feed panic. If anxiety makes it hard to stay still, anxiety.md has coping tools.

The bottom line

Feeling warm during an MRI is normal. The RF pulses deposit a small amount of heat, and scanners track SAR to keep it under set limits. A 3T scan can feel warmer because SAR rises about fourfold for the same sequence. Spot heat, burning, or tingling is not normal. Press the call button at once, and report any skin mark after the scan.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal to sweat during an MRI?

Mild sweating can happen, because your body sheds the heat from the radio wave pulses through blood flow and sweat. The FDA notes that heating is more likely in long exams. Tell the technologist if you feel very hot or unwell.

Can an MRI give you a burn?

Yes, but it is rare. The FDA receives around 300 adverse event reports for MRI scanners and coils each year, and most describe heating or burns. An FDA review found that most of these burns had known causes and were preventable.

Does contrast dye make you feel warm in an MRI?

The FDA patient page says you may feel some coldness when the MRI contrast agent is injected. The warmth people feel during an MRI comes from the radio wave energy, not the contrast. Report any pain or discomfort at the IV site.

Are the MRI heat limits the same at 3T and 1.5T?

Yes. The FDA significant risk guidance gives one set of SAR numbers with no separate figure for 3T. Because SAR at 3T is about four times higher for the same sequence, 3T scans are adjusted to stay under those limits.

Should I keep my arms and legs apart during an MRI?

Yes, unless the technologist positions you differently. An FDA review found burns where skin touched skin, such as inner thighs, calves, and crossed hands. These contacts can form a loop that carries current.
